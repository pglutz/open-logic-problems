## Statement

Let $T$ be a complete first-order theory in a countable language, and let $I(T, \aleph_0)$
denote the number of countable models of $T$ up to isomorphism. Vaught's Conjecture states
that

$$
I(T, \aleph_0) \le \aleph_0 \quad \text{or} \quad I(T, \aleph_0) = 2^{\aleph_0}.
$$

In other words, a complete countable theory cannot have a number of countable models that is
strictly between countable and continuum many.

## Definitions

- **Complete theory**: a theory $T$ such that for every sentence $\varphi$ in its language,
  either $T \vdash \varphi$ or $T \vdash \neg\varphi$.
- **$I(T, \aleph_0)$**: the number of pairwise non-isomorphic countable models of $T$.

## Known Partial Results

- The conjecture is known to hold for theories that are $\omega$-stable (Shelah, Harrington,
  Makkai).
- It is also known for theories of trees and for various other restricted classes.
- Morley proved that the number of countable models of a complete first order theory is either at most $\aleph_0$, exactly $\aleph_1$ or exactly $2^{\aleph_0}$. So any counterexample to Vaught's Conjecture must have exactly $\aleph_1$ countable models.

## Notes

Vaught's Conjecture is widely regarded as one of the central open problems in model theory,
with deep connections to descriptive set theory (via the Topological Vaught Conjecture) and to
the theory of $\Pi^1_1$ equivalence relations.
