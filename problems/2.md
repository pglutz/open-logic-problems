## Statement

Restricted to Turing-invariant functions that are provably total in $\mathsf{ZFC}$ not
constant on a cone of Turing degrees, Martin's Conjecture proposes that every such function
$f$ is, on a cone, equal to an iterate of the Turing jump: there is some ordinal $\alpha$ and a
cone of degrees $\mathbf{x}$ on which

$$
f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^{(\alpha)}.
$$

The conjecture also proposes a well-founded structure ("Martin's Conjecture, part I") on the
Turing-invariant functions ordered by eventual domination on a cone, refining this picture.

## Definitions

- **Turing degree**: the equivalence class of a set $A \subseteq \omega$ under Turing
  equivalence $\le_T$.
- **Cone of degrees**: the set of degrees above some fixed degree $\mathbf{x}_0$; a property
  holds "on a cone" if it holds for all degrees above some $\mathbf{x}_0$.
- **Turing jump**, $\mathbf{x}^{(\alpha)}$: the $\alpha$-th iterate of the Turing jump operator
  applied to the degree $\mathbf{x}$.
- **Turing-invariant function**: a function $f$ on Turing degrees (or on reals, invariant under
  $\equiv_T$) satisfying $f(\mathbf{x}) \geq \mathbf{x}$ on a cone.

## Known Partial Results

- Slaman and Steel proved the conjecture for Borel functions in the "increasing" case.
- The conjecture has been established for order-preserving functions under the Axiom of
  Determinacy in various restricted settings.
- The general case, even for functions of low complexity, remains open.

## Notes

Martin's Conjecture is often described as a precise version of the empirical observation that
"naturally occurring" Turing degrees are always well-ordered by Turing reducibility, despite
the existence of pathological, non-naturally-occurring counterexamples to a naive version of
that statement.
